# US multi-state residency and income allocation, tax year 2026


**State-specific detail:** New York residency in `ny-residency-leaving-new-york` and `ny-it-201-resident-return`, California in `ca-residency-leaving-california` and `ca-540-individual-return`, Florida domicile in `fl-moving-to-florida-domicile`, and the Pennsylvania, Illinois, Michigan, Ohio, Georgia, North Carolina and Massachusetts income tax skills. Use those Guides for their detailed computations, subject to the operative state law. A linked Guide does not override a statute or establish that an unresolved claim is proven.

## Scope and who this is for

This Guide is a method for US individuals who live, move or work in more than one US state. The ordinary civilian routes below do not determine military/spouse elections or Congress-specific exceptions. Those outcomes are non-usable here. Check this point with one of our partner accountants. It covers:

- domicile and statutory residency (the "permanent place of abode plus days" tests);
- part-year returns in a move year;
- sourcing a nonresident's wages by days worked, including the convenience-of-the-employer rule and the remote-worker problem;
- wage reciprocity agreements;
- the resident credit for tax paid to another state;
- the federal bar on source-state tax of retirement income (4 U.S.C. § 114).

**Tax year.** The primary year is **tax year 2026** (returns filed in 2027). The cited form editions are dated **2025**; current statutes and regulations support the continuing rules identified below. A 2025 form is not a verified 2026 filing map. Final 2026 state form lines, campaign dates and year-specific elections are non-usable outputs here until the applicable edition and operative law are established. Check this point with one of our partner accountants. A dated section at the end covers **2025 returns**.

**How to read the state detail.** Residency, sourcing and credits are state law, and every state differs. The key-state table gives each rule with its official source. A state or branch marked **non-usable** has no usable outcome here. Check this point with one of our partner accountants. Where the Skill Library has a state Guide (New York IT-201, New Jersey, Pennsylvania, Connecticut, Delaware, Georgia, Maryland, California 540, Kentucky, Arkansas, Virginia), this Guide supplies the limited rules below.

**Not covered:** business apportionment, pass-through entity taxes, trusts and estates, foreign residency and treaties, US territories, athletes and entertainers, and local taxes. Each state's return computation is in its own Guide.

## Ask the client first

- Which state was the client **domiciled** in on 1 January and on 31 December? If it changed, what is the move date, and what did they do to leave the old state (home sold or let, licence, voter registration, family, business)?
- In every state where they **kept a home** of any kind during the year: was it suitable for year-round living, who used it, and for how much of the year was it kept?
- A **day count** for each such state, with records (calendar, travel, card and toll records). Which state counts part days as full days matters (see the table).
- For each job: where is the employer's office the employee is **assigned to or reports to**? Where was each workday actually spent (office, home, client site, travel)? Did the employer **require** any work outside the office state, in writing?
- Every Form W-2 state box, and which states withheld. Did the employee file a reciprocity or nonresidence certificate with the employer?
- Equity awards (grant, vest and exercise dates, and where the employee worked in between), deferred compensation, severance and bonuses paid after a move.
- Retirement income received after a move: IRA, 401(k), pension, or a nonqualified plan, and how it is paid (lump sum or periodic).
- Rental property, business or partnership income in another state.
- Any return filed in another state, with a copy (most states need it for the credit).

## The method, step by step

1. **Fix the resident state or states for each part of the year.** Start with domicile: a person has only one domicile, and it does not change until they leave the old one and set up a new one. New York requires "clear and convincing evidence" of the change; a certificate of domicile or a new voter registration alone is not enough ([NY residency FAQ](https://www.tax.ny.gov/pit/file/nonresident-faqs.htm)). A domiciliary can still be a nonresident in some states under a narrow exception (see the boundary table).
2. **Test statutory residency in every other state where the client kept a home.** Apply each state’s abode and day requirements separately; a home alone or a day count alone does not supply an unproved state test. Read each state's own wording: "more than 183 days" and "184 days or more" mean the same thing, but "183 days or more" (Maryland) does not, and Virginia's statute looks at the abode for "more than 183 days" rather than at days present. See the key-state table. A person can be a resident of two states at once: the domicile state and a statutory-residence state. Both may assert resident taxation; apply each state’s exclusions and credit rules to the actual income (step 7). A credit is not automatic.
3. **Split the year if the client moved.** Use each state’s own resident-period and nonresident-source recognition rules rather than assuming the receipt date controls every item. New York computes a resident-equivalent tax and then an income ratio, and adds "special accrual" rules for items accrued around a move ([IT-203-I](https://www.tax.ny.gov/pdf/current_forms/it/it203i.pdf)). New Jersey has no single part-year return: file NJ-1040 for the resident period and NJ-1040NR for any New Jersey income as a nonresident ([GIT-6](https://www.nj.gov/treasury/taxation/pdf/pubs/tgi-ee/git6.pdf)).
4. **Source each nonresident item.** Wages: by where the work is physically done, measured in workdays, unless a convenience rule, a reciprocity agreement or a small-presence rule applies (steps 5 and 6). Real property and tangible property: the state where it is. Interest, dividends and gains on securities: generally taxed only by the state of residence, unless the property is used in a business in the source state ([Conn. Gen. Stat. § 12-711](https://www.cga.ct.gov/current/pub/chap_229.htm) is an example). Business and partnership income: by the state's allocation or apportionment rules (outside this Guide).
5. **Apply the convenience-of-the-employer rule where it exists.** In New York, a nonresident whose primary office is in New York has telecommuting days counted as New York workdays unless the employer has established a bona fide employer office at the home ([NY residency FAQ](https://www.tax.ny.gov/pit/file/nonresident-faqs.htm)). Pennsylvania and Delaware apply the same idea in their own words; New Jersey and Connecticut use their own similar-home-state tests; Nebraska’s current statute limits the income sourced under its convenience paragraph to services performed in Nebraska, and Arkansas uses physical presence. Do not infer another state’s rule from an old list. See the convenience-rule table.
6. **Check wage reciprocity.** Under a reciprocity agreement the work state does not tax the wages of a resident of the partner state; the home state taxes them. This table addresses employee compensation. Do not extend a listed wage exemption to self-employment income, rents or K-1 income without the particular agreement’s terms; several rows have additional conditions. See the reciprocity table.
7. **Claim the resident credit in the resident state.** Resident taxation and credits use each resident state’s own law. Proper tax paid elsewhere does not by itself establish a credit: the **resident state’s sourcing rules and exclusions** also matter. Compute the particular state’s cap using final tax, its own income measure and required attachments; withholding is not the credit amount. Do not borrow another state’s denominator or proration. Where the other state taxed wages only because of a convenience rule, the resident state may refuse the credit on those wages (California does: see the credit section).
8. **Protect retirement income.** No state may tax the retirement income of a nonresident, as defined in 4 U.S.C. § 114 (see the retirement section). Other deferred pay after a move is sourced by the state's normal rules.
9. **Prepare the returns and reconcile withholding.** File the resident (or part-year) return in each resident state and a nonresident return in each source state. Claim refunds of wrongly withheld tax in the state that withheld it. Update the employer's withholding forms after a move or a change of work pattern.

## The resident credit for tax paid to other states

- **Who claims it.** Residents (and part-year residents for the resident period). Nonresidents generally cannot, except where a state reverses the credit (below).
- **What it covers.** Income tax of another state on income sourced to and taxed by that state while the person was a resident. New York: "This credit is allowable only for the portion of the tax that applies to income sourced to and taxed by the other taxing authority while you were a New York State resident" ([NY residency FAQ](https://www.tax.ny.gov/pit/file/nonresident-faqs.htm); [IT-112-R-I](https://www.tax.ny.gov/pdf/current_forms/it/it112ri.pdf)).
- **The cap.** Apply the home state’s actual formula. New Jersey compares its proportional cap with the final other-jurisdiction tax after that jurisdiction’s credits, not withholding. Arkansas’s statute separately says: the credit "shall not exceed what the tax would be on the outside income, if added to the Arkansas income, and calculated at Arkansas income tax rates" ([Act 1019](https://www.arkleg.state.ar.us/Home/FTPDocument?path=%2FACTS%2F2021R%2FPublic%2FACT1019.pdf)). The excess is not refunded.
- **Sourcing is judged by the home state's rules.** California: "residents of California may claim a credit only if the income taxed by the other state has a source within the other state under California law", and employee pay has its source "where the services are performed" ([current FTB credit guidance](https://www.ftb.ca.gov/file/personal/credits/other-state-tax-credit.html), linking to the [dated Schedule S instructions](https://www.ftb.ca.gov/forms/2025/2025-540-s-instructions.html)). So a California resident taxed by New York on days worked at home in California gets **no** California credit for that New York tax. California also excludes "Taxes paid to any local government, such as a city or county", so New York City tax is never credited.
- **Reverse credit states.** For ordinary individual returns, California generally reverses the credit for Arizona, Oregon and Virginia; Virginia dual residents and qualifying group nonresident-return participants have distinct exceptions under Schedule S § C. The group-return outcome is non-usable here. Check this point with one of our partner accountants. In the ordinary reverse-credit route, residents of Arizona, Guam, Oregon and Virginia claim the credit on their California nonresident return ([2025 Schedule S instructions](https://www.ftb.ca.gov/forms/2025/2025-540-s-instructions.html)). Virginia's Guide says the same from its side.
- **Dual residents.** Virginia tells an actual (statutory) resident who is domiciled elsewhere to "claim a credit on the return filed in the state of your legal domicile for taxes paid to Virginia" ([VA Form 760 instructions](https://www.tax.virginia.gov/sites/default/files/vatax-pdf/2025-760-instructions.pdf)). Other pairs differ. New York IT-112-R also denies its credit to a dual resident when the other state allows a credit for the total resident tax paid to New York. No simultaneous two-state credit is assumed. Check this point with one of our partner accountants.
- **Attach the other return.** Retain both returns and each state’s prescribed credit attachments. The exact 2026 attachment/line map is non-usable here; do not assume one state’s documentary rule covers all others. Check this point with one of our partner accountants.
- **Constitutional disputes.** The historical *Comptroller v. Wynne* county-credit issue remains a specialist destination, not a substitute credit formula or automatic entitlement in this Guide. Check this point with one of our partner accountants.

## Retirement income: 4 U.S.C. § 114

- "No State may impose an income tax on any retirement income of an individual who is not a resident or domiciliary of such State (as determined under the laws of such State)" ([4 U.S.C. § 114](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCODE-2024-title4/html/USCODE-2024-title4-chap4-sec114.htm)). Residency itself is therefore still decided by each state's own tests. This has applied to amounts received after 31 December 1995.
- **Covered:** qualified trusts under § 401(a) exempt under § 501(a); SEPs (§ 408(k)); § 403(a) annuity plans and § 403(b) contracts; individual retirement plans (§ 7701(a)(37), which includes IRAs); eligible deferred compensation plans under § 457; governmental plans (§ 414(d)); § 501(c)(18) trusts; and uniformed-services retired pay.
- **Nonqualified plans (§ 3121(v)(2)(C)), and written plans in effect immediately before retirement paying for a retired partner’s prior service (the partner must meet § 114(b)(4), including retirement under the partnership agreement), are covered only if** the payment is either (i) part of a series of substantially equal periodic payments, made not less often than annually, for the life or life expectancy of the recipient (or joint lives with the beneficiary) **or** for a period of not less than 10 years; **or** (ii) paid after termination of employment under a plan maintained **solely** to provide benefits above the listed qualified-plan limits (an excess benefit plan).
- **No automatic protection:** an ordinary lump-sum/short-period nonqualified payment outside the statutory excess-benefit route, severance, bonuses and equity pay do not become covered retirement income merely because paid after a move. First classify the actual plan and payment under § 114; any unproved plan classification is non-usable. Check this point with one of our partner accountants. Unprotected pay requires the particular state’s sourcing rules (see equity pay below).
- A state can still tax its own **residents** on this income; a new resident state usually taxes distributions received after the move.

## Equity pay and other deferred wages

- Treat stock options, restricted stock and similar awards as wages earned over an **allocation period**, split by workdays in each state in that period. The period differs by state and by award.
- **New York** (Form IT-203-F): for nonstatutory options with no readily ascertainable value at grant **and not subject to § 409A**, the period is "Grant date to date option vested" (options with a readily ascertainable value at grant are allocated on Form IT-203-B instead); for restricted stock without an § 83(b) election, from the date the stock is received to the earliest of vesting, termination of employment, or sale. Days outside New York count only "because of necessity (not convenience) of the employer" ([IT-203-F-I](https://www.tax.ny.gov/pdf/current_forms/it/it203fi.pdf)).
- **California:** a nonresident's option income is California-source to the extent it is compensation for services performed in California ([FTB Pub. 1100](https://www.ftb.ca.gov/forms/2025/2025-1100-publication.pdf)). The exact allocation period (grant to exercise or grant to vest) is in FTB Publication 1004, has not been proved here for the actual award: the allocation output is **non-usable**. Check this point with one of our partner accountants.
- Other states: no award-specific allocation output is supplied. Check this point with one of our partner accountants. Keep a workday calendar for the whole allocation period of every open award.

## Worked cases

These are illustrations with assumed facts; amounts are rounded to whole dollars.

**Case 1: statutory residency, 183 against 184 days ([NY residency FAQ](https://www.tax.ny.gov/pit/file/nonresident-faqs.htm)).** The client is domiciled in Connecticut and rents a Manhattan apartment suitable for year-round use for the whole year. On 184 days (counting part days) in New York State, of which 184 or more in New York City, they are a New York State and New York City statutory resident: New York taxes all their income, and they must test credit eligibility separately, including the IT-112-R dual-resident exclusion; a credit is not automatic. On 183 days they are a New York nonresident, taxed only on New York-source income, with no New York City personal income tax. The city test is counted separately: 184 State days but only 150 in the city would make them a State resident but a city nonresident. Connecticut, as the domicile state, taxes all income in both cases; any Connecticut credit must satisfy Connecticut’s source and dual-resident rules, with the applicable Schedule 2; the pair-specific final credit is non-usable here. Check this point with one of our partner accountants.

**Case 2: remote worker, New York employer ([IT-203-I](https://www.tax.ny.gov/pdf/current_forms/it/it203i.pdf)).** A Texas resident's assigned office is in New York. Salary $150,000. Of 230 days worked: 30 in the New York office, 180 at home in Texas by choice, and 20 at client sites in Texas that the employer required. The home office meets no bona fide employer office factor. New York workdays = 30 + 180 = 210. New York-source wages = $150,000 x 210 / 230 = $136,957. [Texas Constitution art. VIII § 24-a](https://tcss.legis.texas.gov/Docs/CN/htm/CN.8.htm) bars an individual net-income tax, so there is no Texas resident income-tax credit to offset the New York tax; no entitlement in any other jurisdiction is inferred.

**Case 3: the same job, California resident ([2025 Schedule S instructions](https://www.ftb.ca.gov/forms/2025/2025-540-s-instructions.html)).** The employee works at home in California by choice. New York counts the home days as New York days; California taxes all the wages as a resident, treats pay for work done in California as California-source, and gives no credit for New York tax on those days. Those wages can remain taxed by both states under these rules. The final two-state assessment is non-usable here. Check this point with one of our partner accountants.

**Case 4: Georgia small-presence rule ([Georgia DOR](https://dor.georgia.gov/residency-filing-requirements)).** A Tennessee resident's only Georgia activity is working there as an employee. Total pay from all places $120,000; pay for the Georgia days $4,000. 5% of $120,000 = $6,000. The lesser of $6,000 and $5,000 is $5,000. Georgia pay of $4,000 does not exceed $5,000, so no Georgia return is required. Had Georgia pay been $5,001, that numerical exception would fail and the ordinary nonresident rule would require filing when the federal-return condition is met.

**Case 5: reciprocity ([GIT-6](https://www.nj.gov/treasury/taxation/pdf/pubs/tgi-ee/git6.pdf); [PA-40 instructions](https://www.pa.gov/content/dam/copapwp-pagov/en/revenue/documents/formsandpublications/formsforindividuals/pit/documents/2025/2025_pa-40in.pdf)).** A Pennsylvania resident works in New Jersey and also has a New Jersey rental. New Jersey does not tax the wages; if it withheld, the employee files NJ-1040NR showing the wages in Column A and "0" in Column B, and reports the rent as New Jersey income. Pennsylvania taxes the wages. Proper New Jersey tax actually paid on rental income also taxed by Pennsylvania must be tested under the PA resident-credit rules; tax wrongly withheld on reciprocal wages is recovered from New Jersey rather than assumed creditable. A final rental-credit amount or unverified 2026 form-column map is non-usable. Check this point with one of our partner accountants.

**Case 6: Maryland abode test ([MD nonresident booklet](https://www.marylandtaxes.gov/content/dam/mdcomp/tax/instructions/2025/nonresident-booklet.pdf)).** A Virginia resident works in Maryland. Renting a Maryland flat for four months: the ordinary Virginia-resident wage reciprocity route applies if its other conditions hold; other income is separately tested. Keeping the flat eight months and present 190 days: a Maryland statutory resident. Keeping it eight months but present 150 days: not a statutory resident, but the wage exemption is lost (abode more than six months), so the ordinary Maryland-source wages remain subject to the nonresident rules; the 2025 form route is Form 505, subject to its filing conditions. The residence conclusion uses COMAR’s two-part test, not the booklet sentence that appears to infer residence from the abode alone.

**Case 7: California 546-day absence ([R&TC § 17014](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=RTC&sectionNum=17014)).** A California domiciliary takes a two-year employment contract in Singapore with 30 days of California visits a year. In the second year a share sale produces $250,000 of gains. Because intangible income exceeded $200,000 in a contract year, the 546-day rule does not apply, and residency falls back to the general "temporary or transitory purpose" test; it does not automatically establish residence. The actual disputed residence outcome is non-usable. Check this point with one of our partner accountants.

**Case 8: retirement income after a move ([4 U.S.C. § 114](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCODE-2024-title4/html/USCODE-2024-title4-chap4-sec114.htm)).** A New York retiree moves to Florida in March. If the move ends both New York residence and domicile, qualifying IRA distributions and 401(k) payments received afterwards are protected by § 114; a mailing-address change alone does not establish that condition. A nonqualified plan pays a single lump sum in June; it is not a series of periodic payments and not an excess benefit plan, so § 114 does not protect it, and New York requires its own sourcing analysis. The unproved plan/source/final-tax outcome is non-usable. Check this point with one of our partner accountants.

Cases 2 and 4 recomputed:

```oa-check
name: New York convenience rule, Texas remote worker (2026)
inputs:
  salary: 150000
  office_days: 30
  home_days_by_choice: 180
  required_client_days: 20
steps:
  - total_days: office_days + home_days_by_choice + required_client_days
  - ny_days: office_days + home_days_by_choice
  - ny_wages: round(salary * ny_days / total_days, 0)
expected:
  total_days: 230
  ny_days: 210
  ny_wages: 136957
```

```oa-check
name: Georgia small-presence test (2026)
inputs:
  total_pay: 120000
  georgia_pay: 4000
  dollar_cap: 5000
steps:
  - pct_test: total_pay * 0.05
  - limit: min(pct_test, dollar_cap)
  - margin: georgia_pay - limit
expected:
  pct_test: 6000
  limit: 5000
  margin: -1000
```

A negative margin means Georgia pay does not exceed the limit: no Georgia return.

## When to refuse or refer

Every unresolved outcome in this section and every marked branch elsewhere is **non-usable**. Check this point with one of our partner accountants.

- **Refer** a claim that a home office is a bona fide employer office; it needs the employer's facts.
