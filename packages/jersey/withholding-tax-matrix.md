# Cross-border withholding decision method v1.1

## Scope

For a cross-border services, royalty or interest payment in 2026, establish the
payment's actual source and classification before looking for relief. A payer's
country, a residence certificate, an invoice description or a matrix cell alone
does not establish the amount to withhold. The current branches below cover
selected Canadian services, UK yearly-interest and US personal-services rules.
Other countries and treaty rates require their current country Guide and the
actual operative law and procedure. This method produces a supported review
brief and conditional computation, not an accepted return or professional approval.

**Answer first.** Canada can require **15% of gross non-employment service
payments for work rendered in Canada**, including payments by a nonresident
payer, unless an applicable exclusion or authorized waiver/reduction applies.
UK-source yearly interest can require deduction when paid to a person whose
usual place of abode is outside the UK; there is no universal overseas-interest
zero. US personal-services source generally follows where the work is performed,
not the payer's residence. Each rule has conditions and sources below.

## Ask the client first

1. Identify the legal payer, recipient and any intermediary; entity versus
   individual, residence/status evidence, beneficial claimant and relevant branches.
2. Obtain the contract, rights granted, invoice, gross amount, currency, payment
   date and any gross-up clause. Separate services, software/IP rights and interest.
3. Establish actual work locations and periods, where IP is used and the facts
   relevant to interest source. Identify any employee, permanent-establishment,
   fixed-base or special-industry issue before choosing a rule.
4. Obtain the actual treaty/protocol and effective-period analysis, relevant
   eligibility evidence, required claim forms and any tax-authority waiver or direction.
5. Reconcile prior payments, deductions, remittances and certificates; identify
   the separate residence-country return, credit or refund question and its evidence.

## The method, step by step

1. **Define the payment and period.** Record the actual parties, gross payment,
   legal rights/service and performance locations. A label such as consulting,
   SaaS or licence does not complete classification. Keep mixed components separate.
2. **Determine domestic source and collection first.** Use the relevant country
   rule, including payer/recipient status and exclusions. Canada and the US branches
   below show why payer residence alone is not a worldwide source rule. Do not
   treat the domestic headline as a guaranteed worst-case amount or the final tax.
3. **Evaluate relief on its actual conditions.** For a proposed treaty outcome,
   record the in-force text/protocol, payment article, claimant and any applicable
   ownership, entitlement, anti-abuse, presence and procedural requirements.
   A residence certificate is evidence to assess, not automatic permission to pay
   gross. The Canadian waiver requirement and US personal-services documentation
   below remain distinct from a final treaty-liability conclusion.
4. **Calculate only after the base and rate are supported.** State whether the
   contract amount is gross or a promised net amount. The worked case assumes a
   gross fee: withholding = supported gross base x supported rate; cash to recipient
   = gross fee minus withholding. A gross-up clause needs its own calculation and
   legal base review; do not quietly substitute net for gross.
5. **Complete the country procedure and reconciliation.** Record the source,
   legal basis, relief authority, actual deduction, due date, remittance and report
   evidence. Keep source withholding, final liability, a residence-country credit
   and a source-country refund as separate questions. Do not promise a credit or
   refund without that country's rules and actual payment evidence.
6. **Stop when material support is missing.** Obtain the country Guide and
   qualified review for an unresearched jurisdiction, treaty, IP classification,
   statutory exception or filing branch. An empty cell, old zero, lack of an
   identified PE or the historical archive is not substitute authority.

The old services matrix's Canadian zeros are not a safe rule for work performed
in Canada. Equally, this branch does not impose Canadian services withholding on
every payment merely because the customer is Canadian. Mixed-location work,
expense reimbursements, gross-ups and special waivers need a supported allocation
and their specific rules; the simple case below does not determine them.

### United Kingdom: yearly interest is not universally exempt

Income Tax Act 2007 section 874 applies to a payment of yearly interest arising
in the UK by the specified payers, including payment by any person to another
whose usual place of abode is outside the UK. Deduction is at the savings basic
rate in force for the payment's tax year. Section 874 itself points to statutory
exceptions; a treaty claim may also remove or change the obligation. Establish
UK source, yearly-interest character, payer/payee facts and the actual exception
or relief procedure. This method does not give a universal numeric UK rate or
authorize an unclaimed treaty exemption. [ITA 2007 s874](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2007/3/section/874);
[HMRC SAIM9070](https://www.gov.uk/hmrc-internal-manuals/savings-and-investment-manual/saim9070);
[HMRC SAIM9200](https://www.gov.uk/hmrc-internal-manuals/savings-and-investment-manual/saim9200).

This replaces the old claim that the UK abolished domestic withholding on most
interest. Individual exceptions require evidence; they do not justify every
zero in the old interest table or a reciprocal country-pair shortcut.

### United States: establish where personal services are performed

For personal-services income, performance location generally determines source;
contract location, payment location and payer residence do not decide it. Work
partly inside and partly outside the US requires an accurate allocation on the
facts. Do not turn US-customer/UK-worker residence alone into a zero rate.
[IRS personal-services source](https://www.irs.gov/individuals/international-taxpayers/source-of-income-personal-service-income);
[Publication 515 (2026), Source of Income](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p515).

For compensation paid to a nonresident alien individual for work performed in
the US, the general withholding rule is 30%, unless specifically exempt or
subject to graduated withholding. Employment and independent services must be
distinguished. Form 8233 can be the relevant treaty-exemption form for independent
personal services; W-8BEN is not a universal personal-services treaty solution.
Use the actual status, income and procedural branch. Royalties and interest are
separate source/classification jobs and no US-India or other treaty rate is
validated by this method. [IRS pay for personal services](https://www.irs.gov/individuals/international-taxpayers/pay-for-personal-services-performed);
[Publication 515 (2026), Pay for Personal Services Performed](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p515).

### Other countries, royalties, software and treaty corridors

All old service, royalty, interest, form and corridor rows are retained below
as unverified historical evidence. No worldwide rate, reciprocal zero, standard
software/SaaS characterization, treaty existence or automatic credit is supplied
for the other countries. Obtain the actual country rule, current treaty and
claim procedure before a result. The old EU associated-company directive
shortcut is also held here; being in the EU or being a freelancer does not
establish an exemption through this Guide.

## Worked cases

### Canada: gross fee with and without an authorized waiver

Fictional 2026 payment of CAD 10,000 to an ordinary nonresident independent
consultant for services performed entirely in Canada. It is a gross fee with
no gross-up or reimbursement, no employment relationship, and neither insurer
nor foreign-bank exception applies. With no CRA waiver/reduction letter, the
Regulation 105 deduction is CAD 1,500 and cash to the consultant is CAD 8,500.
This is an advance withholding calculation, not a determination of final tax.
[Regulation 105](https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/regulations/C.R.C.%2C_c._945/section-105.html);
[CRA waiver rule](https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/tax/businesses/topics/payroll/payroll-deductions-contributions/non-resident-deductions/applying-a-waiver-a-reduction-withholding.html).

```oa-check
name: Canadian nonresident service gross fee without waiver
inputs:
  gross_fee: 10000
  regulation_105_rate: 0.15
steps:
  withholding: gross_fee * regulation_105_rate
  cash_to_consultant: gross_fee - withholding
expected:
  withholding: 1500
  cash_to_consultant: 8500
```

Change only one fact: suppose the payer actually holds a valid CRA letter
authorizing no Regulation 105 deduction for this exact payment, recipient and
period, and all its conditions are met. The authorized deduction is then zero
and cash is CAD 10,000, CAD 1,500 more than in the first branch. The assumed
letter, not the recipient's unsupported treaty assertion, causes the difference;
neither branch settles final Canadian tax or reporting.
[CRA authorization and payment-on-account distinction](https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/tax/businesses/topics/payroll/payroll-deductions-contributions/non-resident-deductions/applying-a-waiver-a-reduction-withholding.html).

```oa-check
name: Canadian service fee with exact authorized nil withholding letter
inputs:
  gross_fee: 10000
  authorized_rate_for_this_payment: 0
  ordinary_rate: 0.15
steps:
  authorized_withholding: gross_fee * authorized_rate_for_this_payment
  cash_to_consultant: gross_fee - authorized_withholding
  ordinary_withholding: gross_fee * ordinary_rate
  cash_difference: ordinary_withholding - authorized_withholding
expected:
  authorized_withholding: 0
  cash_to_consultant: 10000
  ordinary_withholding: 1500
  cash_difference: 1500
```

## When to refuse or refer

Refuse to choose a country-pair rate from the historical matrices. Do not file,
remit, certify treaty entitlement or promise a refund from this bounded method.
Refer an unresolved treaty/PE/fixed-base, beneficial-claimant, employment,
software/IP or anti-abuse question with its contracts and source evidence.
Missing material facts produce needs input; missing legal authority produces
a source-review hold, not a guessed default, a zero or a complete result.
A review hold is not permission to pay gross or miss a withholding deadline.

> ## Step 0: Pre-Check Questions [T1]
>
> - **Pre-check questions before using WHT matrix** -- Before using the WHT matrix, you MUST know: 1. Where is the payer (client) tax-resident? This determines the source country and whose domestic WHT law applies. 2. Where is the payee (freelancer) tax-resident? This determines which treaty (if any) applies. 3. What is the nature of the payment? Services (technical/professional/management fees), royalties (software licences, IP), or interest. 4. Does a double tax treaty (DTA) exist between the two countries? If yes, treaty rates may reduce or eliminate WHT. 5. Has the payee obtained a Certificate of Tax Residence (CoR) from their home country? Required to claim treaty benefits. 6. Is there a risk of Permanent Establishment (PE) in the source country? If PE exists, different rules apply (see `permanent-establishment-risk.md`). ([T1])
> - **Stop condition on unknown pre-check items** -- If items 1-3 are unknown, STOP. Do not provide WHT guidance. ([T1])

> ## Step 1: Key Concepts [T1]
>
> - **Domestic WHT Rate** -- The rate a country charges by default on cross-border payments when no treaty applies. This is the "worst case" rate. ([T1])
> - **Treaty WHT Rate** -- The reduced rate available under a bilateral double tax treaty between the payer's and payee's countries. Requires the payee to claim treaty benefits (usually by providing a Certificate of Residence and/or specific forms). ([T1])
> - **Zero-WHT Corridor** -- A country pair where the treaty rate on a specific payment type is 0%. The payee receives the full gross amount with no deduction. ([T1])
> - **Technical/Professional Services** -- Fees for consulting, IT development, accounting, legal, engineering, management, and similar professional services. Some countries (notably India, Brazil) impose WHT on services; many developed countries do not. ([T1])
> - **Royalties** -- Payments for the use of intellectual property, patents, trademarks, copyrights, and importantly: software licences. The classification of software licence fees as "royalties" vs "business profits" is heavily disputed and varies by treaty. ([T1])
> - **Interest** -- Payments for the use of money (loans, deposits, deferred payment arrangements). ([T1])

> ## Step 5: How to Claim Treaty Benefits [T1]
>
> ### General Process
>
> - **General process to claim treaty benefits** -- 1. Obtain a Certificate of Tax Residence (CoR) from your home country's tax authority. This proves you are tax-resident and entitled to treaty benefits. 2. Provide the CoR to the payer before or at the time of payment. Many countries accept CoR for the current or preceding tax year. 3. Complete any required forms specific to the source country (see table below). 4. The payer applies the treaty rate (or 0%) instead of the domestic rate. 5. If WHT is already deducted at the higher domestic rate, the payee can file a refund claim with the source country's tax authority.

> ## PROHIBITIONS
>
> - **Prohibitions list** -- 1. NEVER assume a treaty exists between two countries without verifying. 2. NEVER apply treaty rates without the payee having a valid Certificate of Tax Residence. 3. NEVER advise a client that WHT "doesn't apply" when the source country's domestic law imposes WHT -- the treaty merely reduces the rate, and treaty benefits must be claimed. 4. NEVER ignore the distinction between "business profits" (Art 7, usually no WHT without PE) and "royalties" (Art 12, WHT may apply) when classifying software licence payments. 5. NEVER assume that paying WHT in the source country eliminates the tax obligation in the residence country -- the residence country taxes worldwide income and provides a credit for WHT paid. 6. NEVER provide specific treaty rate advice for country pairs not covered in this matrix -- escalate [T3].

> ## Edge Cases
>
> ### EC1 -- Treaty shopping / Limitation on Benefits (LOB) [T3]
>
> **Situation:** A freelancer is tax-resident in Country A but channels payments through an entity in Country B (which has a more favourable treaty with the source country) to reduce WHT.
> **Resolution:** This is treaty shopping. Many modern treaties (especially US treaties) contain LOB clauses that deny treaty benefits if the recipient is not the "beneficial owner" or does not meet the LOB test. Escalate to tax professional. Do not advise.
>
> ### EC2 -- Permanent Establishment risk [T2]
>
> **Situation:** A freelancer from Country A works on-site in Country B for extended periods. The client in Country B asks whether WHT applies.
> **Resolution:** If the freelancer has created a PE in Country B (see `permanent-establishment-risk.md`), the income may be taxed as business profits attributable to the PE, not as service fees subject to WHT. PE taxation and WHT are mutually exclusive for the same income. Flag for professional review.
>
> ### EC3 -- Beneficial ownership challenged [T2]
>
> **Situation:** A freelancer invoices through a personal services company in a different country. The source country challenges the treaty claim on the basis that the company is not the "beneficial owner" of the income.
> **Resolution:** Beneficial ownership requires that the recipient has the right to use and enjoy the income, not merely act as a conduit. If the personal services company is a genuine entity with substance, treaty benefits should apply. If it is a shell, they may be denied. Flag for professional review.
>
> ### EC4 -- WHT on software-as-a-service (SaaS) subscriptions [T2]
>
> **Situation:** A freelancer pays for a SaaS tool (e.g., cloud software) from a foreign provider. The domestic tax authority of the freelancer's country considers this a "royalty" and requires WHT.
> **Resolution:** The OECD position is that payments for standard SaaS subscriptions (no transfer of copyright, no reproduction rights) are business profits, NOT royalties. However, India (Explanation 4 to Section 9(1)(vi)) and some other countries treat SaaS payments as royalties subject to WHT. Check the source country's domestic law position. Flag T2.
>
> ### EC5 -- No treaty exists between the two countries [T1]
>
> **Situation:** A freelancer in Country A receives payment from a client in Country B, but no bilateral tax treaty exists.
> **Resolution:** The domestic WHT rate of the source country applies in full. No reduction available. The freelancer claims a foreign tax credit in their home country (subject to that country's credit rules). If the domestic rate exceeds the home country's credit capacity, the excess WHT is a real cost.
>
