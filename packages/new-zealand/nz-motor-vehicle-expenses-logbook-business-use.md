# Motor vehicle expenses, logbooks and shareholder-employee vehicles in New Zealand

## Motor vehicle expenses, logbooks and shareholder-employee vehicles in New Zealand

This Guide covers how a business that uses a motor vehicle for both business and private travel establishes its business use proportion, which deduction method applies, and what happens when the vehicle belongs to a close company and is available to a shareholder-employee. Figures are for tax year 2026. Inland Revenue publishes the kilometre rates for this period on its "Kilometre rates 2025-2026" page and describes them as applying for the 2025-2026 income year, so the figures below follow that income year. The logbook period, the close company thresholds and the fringe benefit tax (FBT) motor vehicle limits are stated on separate Inland Revenue pages, each cited under its own table.

## Who this is for

Sole traders and partners in a partnership who use a vehicle for both business and private travel, and who therefore need a business use percentage rather than a full deduction. Inland Revenue states that a sole trader or a partner of a partnership does not pay FBT on business vehicles used privately and instead keeps a logbook and makes adjustments for private use in income tax and GST returns.

Close companies that make one or two motor vehicles available for the private use of shareholder-employees. Inland Revenue states that such a company may elect to file income year FBT returns aligned to its income tax balance date, and may instead elect to opt out of the FBT rules for motor vehicles and apply the income tax rules for vehicle expenditure, making private use adjustments for income tax and GST.

This Guide is not for employers working out FBT on vehicles provided to non-shareholder employees on ordinary quarterly FBT returns, and it does not cover employer reimbursement of an employee who uses their own private vehicle, which Inland Revenue deals with in a separate operational statement (OS 19/04B).

## The method, step by step

1. Identify the structure first, because it decides the regime. Inland Revenue's page on [FBT for motor vehicles](https://www.ird.govt.nz/employing-staff/deductions-from-other-payments/fringe-benefit-tax/types/motor-vehicles/about) states that a sole trader or partner does not pay FBT on a business vehicle used privately and instead adjusts for private use in the income tax and GST returns, while an employer must otherwise pay FBT on employees' private use of motor vehicles unless an exemption applies.
2. For a sole trader or partnership, establish the business use proportion by keeping a logbook for the minimum period in the logbook table, following [Use a logbook](https://www.ird.govt.nz/income-tax/income-tax-for-businesses-and-organisations/types-of-business-expenses/claiming-vehicle-expenses/use-a-logbook). The page directs you to add up the distance travelled for business, work out what proportion that is of the total distance the vehicle travelled, and allocate that proportion of all vehicle costs to the business, including petrol, Warrant of Fitness, maintenance and repairs, insurance and parking.
3. Carry the logbook percentage forward only within the limits in the logbook table, and start a new logbook when the term expires or the proportion of business use moves by more than the change stated in that table.
4. If you use the kilometre rate method instead of actual costs, apply the Tier 1 and Tier 2 rates from [Kilometre rates 2025-2026](https://www.ird.govt.nz/income-tax/income-tax-for-businesses-and-organisations/types-of-business-expenses/claiming-vehicle-expenses/kilometre-rates-2025-2026) to the business portion of the distance bands shown in the kilometre rate table above, reading the cents-per-kilometre amount for the vehicle type off that page. The rates are set for the year by the instrument cited in the second table, [OS 19/04 (KM 2026)](https://www.taxtechnical.ird.govt.nz/operational-statements/2026/os-19-04-km-2026).
5. Note the GST consequence of the method chosen. Inland Revenue's [GST record keeping and adjustments material](https://www.ird.govt.nz/about-us/videos/webinars/gst-workshop/adjustments) states that if you use the kilometre rate you cannot claim GST, and that taxable supply information must be retained for vehicle expenses where GST is claimed.
6. For a close company with a vehicle available to a shareholder-employee, decide between the FBT rules and the income tax rules using [Close companies and fringe benefit tax](https://www.ird.govt.nz/employing-staff/deductions-from-other-payments/fringe-benefit-tax/close-companies). Check the vehicle-count and no-other-benefits conditions in the close company table before opting out.
7. If opting out, make the election in the form and by the deadline in the tables above: a written note that the company is opting out of the FBT rules, included with the income return covering the year the vehicle is acquired or first used for business, as described on [Fringe benefit tax for close companies](https://www.ird.govt.nz/employing-staff/deductions-from-other-payments/fringe-benefit-tax/fringe-benefit-tax-for-close-companies). Cancel any existing FBT registration where the page directs.
8. If staying inside the FBT rules, consider whether to elect income year FBT returns filed at the same time as the income tax return, testing the qualifying conditions and the election deadline in the close company table on the same page.
9. If remaining under FBT, test each exemption on [FBT exemptions for motor vehicles](https://www.ird.govt.nz/employing-staff/deductions-from-other-payments/fringe-benefit-tax/types/motor-vehicles/exemptions), including the work-related vehicle requirements and the weight limit in that table, and keep the records the page requires. The 3-month test period noted in the FBT basics table is the alternative to recording each exempt day.
10. Read the current [Fringe benefit tax guide IR409](https://www.ird.govt.nz/-/media/project/ir/home/documents/forms-and-guides/ir400---ir499/ir409/ir409-2026.pdf?modified=20260401035722) alongside the web pages; it repeats the opt-out rule, the requirement to make private use adjustments for income tax and GST, and the point that FBT is payable where a vehicle is made available for private use even if the employee does not actually use it.

## Ask the client first

- Is the vehicle owned by the individual, the partnership, or the company, and in whose name is it registered?
- Has a logbook been kept, for how many consecutive days, and how long ago was the test period completed?
- Has the pattern of business use changed since the logbook was kept, and by how much?
- How many vehicles are available for the private use of shareholder-employees, and are any other fringe benefits provided?
- Was the vehicle acquired or first used for business in a year whose income tax return has already been filed, and was any opt-out note included with it?
- Is the business GST-registered, and does it intend to claim GST on vehicle running costs?

## When to refuse or refer

- Calculating the FBT payable itself: the valuation bases, FBT rates and quarterly or income year return calculations are not covered here and no FBT rate figures are stated in this Guide.
- The cents-per-kilometre amounts for a specific vehicle type: they must be read directly from the Inland Revenue kilometre rates page cited above and are not reproduced in this draft.
- Employer reimbursement of an employee using their own private vehicle, which Inland Revenue addresses in operational statement OS 19/04B rather than the pages used here.
- Depreciation, disposal, or adjustments on sale of a business vehicle, and lease or finance arrangements.
- Detailed GST apportionment and change-of-use adjustments beyond the point that GST cannot be claimed where the kilometre rate is used.
- Work-related vehicle daily record-keeping disputes, weekend or statutory holiday availability arguments, and unclassified benefit treatment for vehicles above the weight limit in the exemptions table.
- Charities and non-close-company employers, whose position the pages used here address only in passing.
