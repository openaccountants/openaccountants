# Renting your home to your own business for up to 14 days (the Augusta rule, section 280A(g)), tax year 2026

> **Source-cited draft.** Built from 26 U.S.C. 162, 280A, 707 and 6001, IRS Pubs. 527, 542 and 587, IRS Topic 415, the IRS single-member LLC page, the Instructions for Forms 1099-MISC and 1099-NEC and Rev. Proc. 2025-32, read on 2026-11-13. No accountant has reviewed it. All outputs must be reviewed by a qualified tax professional before filing.

**Answer first.** If you use a home as a residence in 2026 and it is actually rented for **less than 15 days** in the year (14 or fewer, counting every renter), the rent is left out of your income and you deduct no rental expenses (section 280A(g)). The plan is worth something only when a **separate taxpayer** pays: your S corporation, C corporation or partnership, which deducts a **fair rent** for **real business use** under section 162. A **sole proprietor or a disregarded single-member LLC gets nothing**: there is no separate payer. At 15 or more rental days the exclusion is lost for the whole year. The payer files Form 1099-MISC when rent paid reaches **USD 2,000** (the 2026 threshold).

In scope: a business owner who is thinking of renting their home to their own business for meetings in 2026, and the adviser: who the plan can work for, the deduction on the business side, the day count, fair rent, the home office conflict, Form 1099-MISC and the records.

## Ask the client first

1. **How is the business taxed:** sole proprietor, single-member LLC (disregarded or electing corporation), partnership, S corporation or C corporation? Who owns the home?
2. **On how many days in 2026 will the home be rented to anyone,** including short-stay platforms, family lets and the business?
3. **What business events will take place at the home,** who attends, and why at the home rather than the office or a venue?
4. **What would a similar venue charge** for the same use, and can you get dated quotes before the first event?
5. **Does the business already pay you rent or a reimbursement for a home office,** and is any room claimed as exclusively used for business?
6. **Is it your main home, a second home, or a property you also rent out** during the year?

### Steps

1. **Who owns the home and who pays.** The rent must pass from a separate taxpayer (a C corporation, an S corporation, or a partnership, including an LLC taxed as one of these) to the individual owner. A sole proprietorship or a disregarded single-member LLC is not a separate payer: stop ([IRS: single-member LLCs](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/single-member-limited-liability-companies)).
2. **Residence test.** The owner must use the home as a residence for 2026 under section 280A(d)(1). A main home lived in all year passes; a second home or a mostly-rented property needs the day test.
3. **Count every day the dwelling unit is actually rented in 2026, to anyone.** At 15 or more, section 280A(g) does not apply to any of the year's rent: use the ordinary rental rules.
4. **Payer's deduction** under [26 U.S.C. 162(a)](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCODE-2024-title26/html/USCODE-2024-title26-subtitleA-chap1-subchapB-partVI-sec162.htm): each day must be an ordinary and necessary expense of carrying on the business, actually paid, for a real business event at the home.
5. **Fair rent**, with comparable quotes in the file before the event ([Pub. 527](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-pdf/p527.pdf)). For a corporation, rent unreasonably above what the shareholder would charge a stranger may be treated, as to the excess, as a distribution ([Pub. 542](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-pdf/p542.pdf)).
6. **Home office conflict:** an ongoing rental of a room to the owner's employer, or a room claimed as exclusively used for business, changes the result (section 280A(c)(1) and (c)(6)).
7. **Form 1099-MISC** from the payer for rents at or above USD 2,000 ([Instructions for Forms 1099-MISC and 1099-NEC](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-pdf/i1099mec.pdf)).
8. **The owner leaves the rent off the return** and files no Schedule E for it ([Pub. 527](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-pdf/p527.pdf)). Mortgage interest, property tax and qualified casualty losses stay on Schedule A as normal.
9. **Keep the records** listed below for every rental day ([26 U.S.C. 6001](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCODE-2024-title26/html/USCODE-2024-title26-subtitleF-chap61-subchapA-partI-sec6001.htm)).

## Boundary and exception table

### The rule in the statute

Section 280A(g) says that, notwithstanding any other provision of section 280A or section 183, if a dwelling unit is used during the year by the taxpayer as a residence and is actually rented for less than 15 days during the year, no deduction otherwise allowable because of the rental use is allowed, and the income from that use is not included in gross income under section 61. Both halves apply together: the owner gets the exclusion AND loses rental deductions for those days, and cannot choose to report the rent in order to deduct rental expenses. The IRS says the same: rent a home less than 15 days and the rental is not reported on Schedule E (Pub. 527); "don't report any of the rental income and don't deduct any expenses as rental expenses" ([IRS Topic 415](https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc415)).

- **Dwelling unit:** a house, apartment, condominium, mobile home, boat or similar property, with appurtenant structures; not a portion used exclusively as a hotel, motel, inn or similar establishment (section 280A(f)(1)).
- **Residence:** personal-use days exceed the greater of 14 days or 10% of fair-rental days. A day is personal use if, for any part of the day, the owner, a co-owner or a family member of either uses it, among other cases (section 280A(d)(2)).
- **The tenant:** the statute says nothing about who the tenant is and gives the tenant no deduction; that is section 162.

### Who it works for

| Payer | Result |
| --- | --- |
| C corporation or S corporation renting the shareholder's home | A separate taxpayer. It can deduct rent under section 162(a)(3) ("rentals or other payments required to be made as a condition to the continued use or possession, for purposes of the trade or business, of property to which the taxpayer has not taken or is not taking title or in which he has no equity"), if also ordinary and necessary under section 162(a). The shareholder excludes the rent if the day count and residence test are met |
| Partnership (including a multi-member LLC taxed as one) renting a partner's home | A transaction with a partner not acting as a member is treated as with a non-partner, except as section 707 provides ([26 U.S.C. 707(a)(1)](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCODE-2024-title26/html/USCODE-2024-title26-subtitleA-chap1-subchapK-partI-sec707.htm)); the deduction is still tested under section 162 |

The exclusion belongs to "the taxpayer" who uses the unit as a residence and from whose rental use the income comes. If the home is owned by someone else (a trust, an entity, or one spouse alone), refer.

### Who it does not work for

| Situation | Why |
| --- | --- |
| Sole proprietor | Same taxpayer on both sides; nothing is paid to anyone else. Section 162(a)(3) rent is for property "to which the taxpayer has not taken or is not taking title or in which he has no equity", and the owner holds title. Moving money from the business account and calling it rent creates no deduction and no rent to exclude. The home-based deduction open to a sole proprietor is the section 280A(c)(1) home office ([Pub. 587](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-pdf/p587.pdf)) |
| Single-member LLC that is disregarded | Treated as disregarded unless it files Form 8832 to be a corporation; same answer as a sole proprietor. One that elected corporate treatment falls under "Who it works for" |
| Home rented 15 days or more in the year, counting all tenants | Exclusion lost for the whole year, not just the days above 14 |
| Owner who does not use the unit as a residence that year | A property held only for rent does not qualify |
| Business pays for days with no real business use (a family gathering, a household party, a meeting that did not happen) | Not ordinary and necessary under section 162(a); nothing to deduct. Whether the owner can still exclude the payment is not settled on the pages read: Pub. 542 says rent unreasonably above a stranger's rate "may be treated as a distribution to the shareholder", and a payment for a day with no business use is not plainly rent. Refer |

### The 14-day count

- **Count all renters.** Days the unit is actually rented, not days rented to the business: short-stay platform nights, a film shoot, a weekend let to relatives all count.
- **Days rented below fair rent.** Section 280A(g) says "actually rented", not rented at a fair rental. For the residence test, section 280A(d)(2)(C) treats a day as personal use "unless for such day the dwelling unit is rented for a rental which, under the facts and circumstances, is fair rental". No page read says whether such a day also counts toward the 15. This skill counts every rented day, the cautious reading.
- **A cliff.** At 15 or more rental days section 280A(g) does not apply at all: all the rent, the business rent included, is income under the ordinary rules, with expenses divided between rental and personal use (Pub. 527). No partial exclusion.
- **Per tax year, per unit.** The count restarts each year; a second home has its own count and residence test.
- **Part days.** The statute counts days, not hours. Treat any day the unit is rented for any part of the day as one rental day.
- **Available but not rented** days are not rental use (Pub. 527).

### The business side: ordinary, necessary and a fair rent

Section 162(a) allows "all the ordinary and necessary expenses paid or incurred during the taxable year in carrying on any trade or business". For each day the file should show why the business needed a meeting space, why at the home, and what happened there. A fair rental price is generally what a person not related to you would be willing to pay, and is not fair if substantially less than rents for similar properties in the area (Pub. 527). Pub. 527 asks of a comparison property: same purpose, approximately the same size, approximately the same condition, similar furnishings, similar location? If any answer is no, the properties are probably not similar. Compare with meeting or event space offering what the home actually offered that day (capacity, rooms, equipment, catering if included), not a ballroom. Rent set too high: for a corporation, "the excessive part of the rent may be treated as a distribution to the shareholder" (Pub. 542); how an S corporation treats an excess is for the adviser.

### Related-party optics

The owner signs both sides. A lease or invoice written by the owner shows that money moved; it does not show that the business needed the day or that a stranger would have paid the rent, the two section 162 questions. Evidence made by others (venue quotes, attendees) carries the weight. For an S corporation the rent is not a substitute for salary; reasonable compensation is a separate test (`us-s-corp-election-decision`).

### The home office conflict

Section 280A(a) disallows deductions for a dwelling unit used as a residence by an individual or an S corporation, except as the section provides. The home office exception in section 280A(c)(1) covers only the part "exclusively used on a regular basis" as the principal place of business, a place to meet patients, clients or customers, or a separate structure used in the business.

- **A room used for meetings and family life is not exclusively used for business** (Pub. 587). The Augusta rental creates no home office deduction for the rooms used.
- **Ongoing rent of a home office to the owner's own corporation.** Section 280A(c)(6) denies the home office and rental exceptions for any item attributable to renting the unit, or a portion of it, to the taxpayer's employer while the taxpayer uses it as an employee. Section 280A(c)(6) does not itself touch the exclusion, because section 280A(g) applies "notwithstanding any other provision of this section". The risk is the day count: on this skill's reading every day of office rent is a day the unit is "actually rented", so an ongoing monthly rent takes the home to 15 or more days and the exclusion is lost for the year for the whole home. The statute says "dwelling unit (or any portion thereof)" in (c)(6) and "such unit (or portion thereof)" in (d)(1), but only "such dwelling unit" in (g); whether renting one room counts is not settled on the pages read, and this skill treats it as counting.
- **One route for an S corporation owner's home office** is an accountable plan reimbursement, not rent (`us-hiring-your-children-and-accountable-plans`).

### Form 1099-MISC

A corporation or partnership paying rent in its trade or business files Form 1099-MISC, box 1, for "all types of rents" at or above USD 2,000, including "Real estate rentals paid for office space" (Instructions for Forms 1099-MISC and 1099-NEC). The exception for payments to corporations does not help, because the payee is the individual homeowner. The instructions do not mention section 280A(g), and nothing in them excuses a payer because the payee may exclude the rent. The owner still leaves the rent out of income under section 280A(g)(2). No IRS page read says how to show the excluded amount so that IRS matching does not flag the form; keep the file that proves the day count.

### Records that prove each test

Section 6001 requires the records the Secretary prescribes and lets the IRS require records sufficient to show liability. No IRS page lists records for this rule; keep one file per year:

| Test | Records |
| --- | --- |
| Business purpose (section 162) | For each day: agenda, attendee list with roles, minutes or notes; for a corporation, a board resolution or minutes approving the arrangement |
| Fair rent | Comparable quotes dated before the event, from venues similar in purpose, size, condition, furnishings and location, and a note of why the rate fits |
| Payment | Invoice from the owner for each event, payment from the business bank account to the owner's personal account, the business's books |
| Day count | A calendar of every day the home was rented to anyone in the year, including platform bookings |
| Residence | Evidence the home was the owner's residence (for a second home, a personal-use day log) |
| Information return | Form 1099-MISC issued by the payer when USD 2,000 is reached |

### Defaults when a fact is missing

| Missing fact | Default, and flag it |
| --- | --- |
| Entity type | Treat as a sole proprietor (no deduction) until the election is shown |
| Other rental days | Assume the home may be rented elsewhere; ask for platform statements |
| Comparable quotes | No rent figure until quotes dated before the event exist |
| Home office rent or reimbursement | Ask; an ongoing rent can break the day count |

### Red flags to raise

| Flag | Why it matters |
| --- | --- |
| Rent of 14 days at a high daily rate with no quotes | Excess may be a distribution; deduction at risk |
| Short-stay listing on the same home | Day count may pass 14 |
| Meetings with only family members | Business purpose doubtful |
| Monthly home office rent to the same corporation | Section 280A(c)(6) and the day count |

## Worked cases

**Case 1, the ordinary case and the boundary.** Dana owns all the shares of an S corporation and lives in her house all year. The corporation holds six planning and training days at the house, eight staff each. Before the first event her adviser gets three written quotes from local venues for a room for eight with a screen and lunch: USD 1,400, USD 1,500 and USD 1,700 a day. The corporation pays USD 1,500 a day, USD 9,000 in total. The house is rented to no one else. The corporation deducts USD 9,000 if each day was ordinary and necessary; Dana excludes USD 9,000; the corporation issues her a Form 1099-MISC because USD 9,000 reaches the USD 2,000 threshold. Boundary: if Dana also lets the house on a short-stay platform for 10 days in August, rental days are 16, not less than 15, so section 280A(g) does not apply for the year and both the USD 9,000 and the platform rent are rental income with expenses divided between rental and personal days. With 8 platform days instead, the count is 14 and all the rent, the platform rent included, is excluded.

```oa-check
name: Augusta rule day count and exclusion (2026)
inputs:
  business_days: 6
  daily_rent: 1500
  platform_days_a: 10
  platform_days_b: 8
steps:
  - business_rent: business_days * daily_rent
  - days_a: business_days + platform_days_a
  - excluded_if_a: business_rent * (1 - min(floor(days_a / 15), 1))
  - days_b: business_days + platform_days_b
  - excluded_if_b: business_rent * (1 - min(floor(days_b / 15), 1))
expected:
  business_rent: 9000
  days_a: 16
  excluded_if_a: 0
  days_b: 14
  excluded_if_b: 9000
```

**Case 2, rent set too high.** Lee owns a C corporation that pays Lee USD 3,000 a day for five meeting days at Lee's home, USD 15,000. Lee's own quotes show similar space at USD 1,500 a day, so a fair rent is USD 7,500 and the excess is USD 7,500, which may be treated as a distribution to Lee rather than rent (Pub. 542). Whether the corporation deducts the fair part depends on section 162 as in Case 1.

```oa-check
name: Excess rent to a shareholder (2026)
inputs:
  days: 5
  rent_paid_per_day: 3000
  fair_rate_per_day: 1500
steps:
  - rent_paid: days * rent_paid_per_day
  - fair_rent: days * fair_rate_per_day
  - excess_possible_distribution: max(rent_paid - fair_rent, 0)
expected:
  rent_paid: 15000
  fair_rent: 7500
  excess_possible_distribution: 7500
```

**Case 3, the sole proprietor (refuse).** Sam is a sole proprietor and owns his home. He moves USD 9,000 from the business account to his personal account and labels it "rent for six meetings". No deduction: Sam holds title, and section 162(a)(3) rent is for property "to which the taxpayer has not taken or is not taking title or in which he has no equity". No separate payer and no income to exclude. Same answer for a single-member LLC that has not elected corporate treatment.

## Filing and payment

The payer deducts the rent on its own 2026 return (Form 1120-S, 1120 or 1065) and files Form 1099-MISC box 1 to the owner and the IRS by the 2027 due dates in the Form 1099-MISC instructions (see `us-1099-nec-issuance`). The owner reports nothing on Schedule E for the excluded rent and files the 2026 Form 1040 by **15 April 2027**. Keep the year's file with the return.

## Completion checklist

- Payer is a separate taxpayer (corporation or partnership), not a sole proprietor or disregarded LLC.
- Residence test met for 2026.
- Every rental day in 2026, to anyone, counted: total 14 or fewer.
- Each day has a business purpose record; comparable quotes dated before the event support the rate.
- No ongoing home office rent to the same business, or the conflict referred.
- Form 1099-MISC issued if rent reaches USD 2,000.
- Rent left off the owner's return; Schedule A items unchanged.
